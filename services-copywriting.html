Strategy

Copywriting

Words that carry a concept, in English and Arabic — whichever the audience actually reads in.

Short, declarative sentences over ornamental ones. A concept has to survive translation, not just be translated — "Find Your Business Match" works as a dating-app conceit in English and Arabic because the mechanic itself is universal, not because the words map one-to-one. Bilingual work isn't "write it twice" — it's deciding, platform by platform and audience by audience, which language actually gets read, and writing that one properly instead of running both at half effort.

What's included in copywriting work?

Any words a brand puts in front of people, written in English, Arabic, or both.

  • Brand lines and slogans. The short phrases that carry a concept. "Find Your Business Match" for BiznEX. «توصل الفلوس» (the money gets there) for Paytabs, an agency-side proposal (proposed, not implemented), written in simple everyday Egyptian Arabic.
  • Website copy. Headlines, page structure in words, and service pages. I wrote website copy for the Egypt Retail Summit.
  • Social content. Captions, post series and content calendars, written for the platform and the audience reading it.
  • Brochures. For Seniors Hotel and ADVIDA Compound.
  • Video and ad scripts. The BiznEX Holding TV ad, including voiceover direction, and the Seniors Hotel ad, which I also directed on set.
  • Naming. Ayla Kitchen, built on the Arabic word for family, and Everybear Agency.

Short lines take the longest. A slogan has to carry the whole idea in a few words, so I write many and keep one.

How do you write in two languages?

I write both myself. Arabic isn't a translation of the English.

Each language gets its own first draft, written for the people who will read it. At Magnaite, the English content on LinkedIn and Instagram is polished case-study writing. The Arabic content on X and TikTok is written to feel native to those platforms. Same company, same position, two different registers.

Within Arabic, the dialect matters too. A Saudi decision-maker, an Egyptian consumer and a Kuwaiti parent don't read the same way. I pick the register before I write the first line.

How does the work run?

I write the copy. A supervised team designs around it.

Copy starts from the position, so the first step is always to agree what the brand is saying. Then I draft, we review, and I revise. When design starts, I check the layouts against the copy so the words don't get cut to fit a box. Nothing goes out without that final read.

If the copy is for a campaign, we agree the KPIs first: what the words are supposed to make people do.

Who is copywriting for?

It fits best when the words are the problem.

  • A brand whose Arabic content reads like translated English, and the audience can tell.
  • A technical product that buyers don't understand from the current copy. Mawred HR needed an HR and payroll platform explained, in Arabic, to non-technical decision-makers.
  • A launch that needs a name, a line and the first wave of content written together.
  • A website or brochure that says a lot and persuades nobody.

What do you get first?

A short set of core lines in the language your audience reads, before any long-form writing.

That usually means the position in one sentence, a headline or slogan, and a few sample captions or page openers in the voice we've agreed.

If those lines are right, the rest of the copy has something to hang on. If they aren't, we find out early.

Questions people ask

Do you write Arabic copy yourself?

Yes. I'm fluent in Arabic and English and write both myself, without sending Arabic out for translation.

Which Arabic dialects do you write in?

I've written for audiences in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Kuwait, and choose the register per audience, from formal Arabic to everyday Egyptian Arabic.

Can you write copy for a website someone else is designing?

Yes. I can write the copy and review the layouts with your designer so the words and the design agree.
