Employer branding for small companies: start with one real reason.

Big companies have employer branding teams. Small companies have a job post written in a hurry. That gap is also an opportunity.

By Reem Dissoky · Published 30 October 2026 · Updated 30 October 2026

What is employer branding?

Employer branding is how your company presents itself to the people you want to hire: why someone good should work here, said clearly and consistently wherever a candidate looks.

Most companies put real care into what they say to customers. Then the careers side runs on autopilot. A generic job description. A careers page with no voice. Nothing a good candidate couldn't find word for word on a competitor's site.

Do small companies need employer branding?

More than big ones. A large employer can lean on a name people already know. A 40-person company in Riyadh or Cairo has to explain itself from zero, usually in one job post, to a candidate comparing it with five others.

Step 1: Find one real reason

Not five values. One reason someone good would choose you. It has to be true, specific, and something a competitor can't copy-paste.

Ask your best people why they stayed. Not why they joined. Listen for the thing they say without prompting. That's usually it.

Step 2: Say it the same way everywhere

  • The job post. The reason in the first three lines, before the requirements.
  • The careers page. One page, one message, real photos of real people.
  • LinkedIn. The company page and how your team talks about work.
  • The interview. The hiring manager should be able to say it in their own words.

Step 3: Let employees carry it

Candidates trust a team member's post more than a company post. Make it easy for people to share what work is actually like, without scripting them into sounding like an ad.

Step 4: Write it in the candidate's language

In Saudi Arabia and Egypt, that often means Arabic and English, with different registers for different roles. A translated job post reads like one.

Step 5: Measure what HR already measures

Applications per role. Quality of shortlist. Offer acceptance rate. Time to hire. If the employer brand is working, those move. Likes on the careers post are not the goal.

What should the first rewritten job post look like?

It should lead with the reason, then the role, then the requirements, in that order.

  1. Open with the reason. One or two lines on why someone good would choose you. The thing your best people said without prompting.
  2. Describe the work, not the title. What this person will actually do in a normal week, and who they will work with.
  3. Keep the requirements honest. Separate what is essential from what is nice to have. A long wish list filters out good people who read it literally.
  4. Say what happens next. How many interview stages, and who the candidate will meet. Small companies can win on clarity here.

Pick the role you hire for most often and rewrite that post first. Then reuse the structure.

What gets in the way?

Usually, not the budget. Three things come up more often.

  • The reason isn't true yet. If the team wouldn't recognise it, candidates will find out in the interview. Fix the reason before you publish it.
  • Nobody owns it. Recruitment sits with HR, the careers page with marketing, the interview with the manager. Name one person who keeps the message consistent.
  • It stops after the offer. The first weeks at work either confirm the reason or quietly undo it. New hires talk.

Start small. Keep it true. Then say it everywhere.

Questions people ask

How much does employer branding cost for an SME?

The foundation costs more thinking than money: one clear reason, rewritten job posts and a real careers page. Production budget can come later.

What is an employer value proposition (EVP)?

The core reason someone should work for you: what you offer, what you expect, and what makes that combination different. Employer branding is how you communicate it.

How do you measure employer branding?

With hiring numbers: applications per role, shortlist quality, offer acceptance rate and time to hire.
