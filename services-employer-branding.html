Strategy

Employer Branding

Making a company's pitch to talent as deliberate as its pitch to customers.

Most companies put real care into customer-facing messaging and let everything talent-facing run on autopilot — a generic job description, a careers page with no voice, nothing a good candidate couldn't find word-for-word on a competitor's site. My approach treats "why work here" with the same rigor as any other brand position: one real reason, stated plainly, repeated consistently across job posts, the careers page, and how current employees are actually asked to talk about the place.

What's included in employer branding work?

  • The one real reason. Found by talking to the people who stayed, not by writing values on a wall.
  • Job posts. Rewritten so the reason comes before the requirements, in English and Arabic.
  • Careers page. One clear message, real people, a short path to applying.
  • Recruitment content. LinkedIn and social content that shows what working there is actually like.
  • Hiring-side metrics. Applications per role, shortlist quality, offer acceptance, time to hire.

Read more: employer branding for small companies.

How do you work on employer branding?

I find the real reason people work there, write it down, and then make every hiring touchpoint say it.

The research is mine. I talk to the people who stayed, the people who recently joined, and the managers who hire. I read the current job posts and the careers page the way a candidate would. The reason usually turns up in what employees say casually, not in the values statement.

I use the same habit in customer work. At Goot Resorts, I sampled sales calls weekly and visited the resort as a guest, because what people actually say is better evidence than what a brief says.

Then I write the position and the job-post structure myself, in English and Arabic. A supervised team handles design for the careers page and recruitment content, and media buying if job posts need paid reach. I check everything before it goes live.

We agree the hiring metrics before we start, so we can see what changed.

Who is employer branding for?

It suits companies where hiring has become harder than it should be.

  • Roles stay open for weeks, or the right people don't apply at all.
  • Candidates accept, then turn down the offer, and nobody is sure why.
  • The company is growing fast and hiring in a new market, like a Saudi expansion, where nobody knows its name yet.
  • The customer brand is strong and the hiring side looks like it belongs to a different company.
  • A small company competing for people against bigger, better-known employers. More on that in employer branding for small companies.

What do you get first?

The one real reason, written in a sentence, and one job post rewritten around it.

You also get a short note on what I heard in the interviews, so the reason is backed by what your own people said and not by my opinion.

That gives you something concrete to test on a live role before the careers page or content is rebuilt. If the reason holds up with candidates, the rest of the work follows from it.

How does it connect to the rest of the brand?

Directly. Employer branding is brand communication aimed at candidates instead of customers.

The job posts need copywriting in the language candidates read. Recruitment content runs on the same social channels as the brand, so the tone has to match. I plan them together so the company doesn't sound like two different places.

One position, two audiences.

Questions people ask

What is employer branding?

Employer branding is how a company presents itself to the people it wants to hire: one clear reason to work there, said consistently on job posts, the careers page, LinkedIn and in interviews.

Do you offer employer branding in Saudi Arabia?

Yes. I work on employer branding in Saudi Arabia and Egypt, in English and Arabic.

How do you measure employer branding?

With hiring numbers: applications per role, shortlist quality, offer acceptance rate and time to hire.
